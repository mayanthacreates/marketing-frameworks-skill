# Pygmalion Effect (expectancy effect)

**What it is:** Higher expectations from a leader, teacher or manager can lead to better performance, because the expectations change how that person behaves toward others (more support, feedback, challenge and warmth). The reverse, low expectations dragging performance down, is called the **Golem effect**.

Named after the Greek myth (and Shaw's play). The classic study is Rosenthal & Jacobson's *Pygmalion in the Classroom* (1968): teachers told certain pupils were "about to bloom" saw those pupils gain more IQ points.

## Where it shows up in marketing work

- **Managing teams, freelancers, creators and sales reps:** how you brief and talk to people shapes their output.
- **Agency-client relationships:** clients who expect great work tend to give better briefs and feedback, which produces better work.
- **Customer expectations:** framing customers as capable ("you've got this") helps onboarding and self-serve adoption. (This is a related expectation idea, not the original effect.)

## How to apply it

1. Set specific, high but believable expectations ("I think you can lift CTR to 2%").
2. Back them with resources: clear briefs, time, feedback, tools.
3. Give frequent, specific feedback and recognise progress.
4. Watch for Golem behavior in yourself: fewer chances, less information, curt feedback for people you've written off.

## Common mistakes

- Expectations without support (pressure, not Pygmalion).
- Fake praise. People detect it and trust drops.

## Evidence

Real but small and contested. The 1968 study was heavily criticised for its methods. Later reviews (for example Jussim & Harber, 2005) conclude teacher expectation effects exist but are generally modest and fade over time. Workplace studies (Eden and others) find positive effects, strongest for new or disadvantaged groups. Present it as a nudge on management style, not a magic lever.

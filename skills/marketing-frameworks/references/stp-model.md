# STP Model (Segmentation, Targeting, Positioning)

**What it is:** The core process of marketing strategy. Split the market into groups, choose which groups to serve, then decide how you want those groups to see you versus the alternatives. Segmentation as an idea goes back to Wendell Smith (1956); the STP process is the backbone of Philip Kotler's textbooks.

## The three steps

**1. Segmentation - split the market into groups that respond differently.**
Common bases: demographic (age, income), geographic (city, climate), psychographic (values, lifestyle), behavioral (usage rate, occasion, loyalty, benefit sought). Behavioral and benefit-based segments are usually the most useful for marketing decisions.

**2. Targeting - choose which segments to serve.**
Score each segment on size, growth, profitability, reachability and your ability to win it. Pick one or a few. Strategies range from undifferentiated (one offer for all) to differentiated (offers per segment) to concentrated (one niche).

**3. Positioning - decide the place you want to own in the target's mind.**
Template: *For [target], [brand] is the [category] that [key benefit] because [reason to believe], unlike [main alternative].*
A perceptual map (two axes that buyers care about, competitors plotted) helps find open space.

## How to apply it

1. List 3-6 candidate segments with what each one needs.
2. Score them in a table and pick the target.
3. Write the positioning statement and test it against real competitors: is it true, different and important to that segment?
4. Feed the result into the 4 Ps (every P should support the position).

## Worked example

A gym: segments include students, busy professionals, retirees, serious lifters. Target: professionals 28-45 who work long hours. Positioning: "For busy professionals, FitNow is the gym that gets you a full workout in 30 minutes, with coached express classes every half hour from 6am, unlike big-box gyms where you plan your own session."

## Common mistakes

- Segments defined only by demographics, with no difference in needs.
- Targeting "everyone" (no trade-off made).
- Positioning on something competitors also claim ("quality", "great service").

## Evidence

Foundational strategy process, not an empirical claim. Its value depends on the quality of the customer research behind it.

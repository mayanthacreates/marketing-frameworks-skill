# Rule of 7

**What it is:** A marketing rule of thumb that a prospect needs to see or hear a message about seven times before they act (buy, sign up, call). The usual origin story is that 1930s film studios found people needed to see a poster about seven times before buying a ticket.

## What it gets right

- One exposure rarely converts. Repetition builds recognition, familiarity and trust (the mere-exposure effect, Zajonc 1968, is well established).
- Multiple touchpoints across channels (ad, post, email, review, word of mouth) are normal before a purchase, especially for higher-priced items.

## What it gets wrong

- **There is nothing special about 7.** The right frequency depends on price, category, how memorable the creative is, and how warm the audience is. Herbert Krugman's "Why Three Exposures May Be Enough" (1972, *Journal of Advertising Research*) argued the work is done by three psychological stages (curiosity, recognition, reminder), not a magic count; other studies find different numbers for different contexts.
- Repetition has diminishing returns, and too much causes ad fatigue and annoyance (wear-out).

## How to apply it (the useful version)

1. Plan a *sequence* of touchpoints across the funnel, not one hero post.
2. Use frequency caps in paid media and watch for falling CTR and rising CPA as signs of wear-out.
3. Vary the creative so repeated exposure stays fresh.
4. Retarget warm audiences, who need fewer touches than cold ones.
5. Measure your own path to conversion (analytics, "how did you hear about us") instead of assuming 7.

## Worked example

Launching a new cafe: an opening teaser on social, a local influencer visit, a Google Business Profile with photos, a flyer at the gym next door, a launch-week offer email to the waitlist, then reviews appearing. That is six or seven natural touches, spread over two weeks, across different channels.

## Evidence

Folklore. The 1930s story has no primary source that anyone cites. The underlying idea (repetition and multiple touchpoints matter) is sound. The specific number is not. Say so when a user treats it as a law.
